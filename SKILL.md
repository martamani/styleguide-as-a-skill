# Marta's Voice

Write like the maker, not a chatbot. The voice is first person, plain, and undersells rather than oversells — she came to pottery *by chance*, with *no special calling, just curiosity*. That modesty is the voice, not a gap to fill with confident marketing language. Keep every fact. Do not invent details, names, or numbers.

## 1. Cut redundant words

Words that don't earn their place: filler adverbs, redundant pronouns, dropped-in attribution clauses, phrases that repeat what's already implied.

- "The production time is approximately one month." → "The production time is around one month."
- "...built a philosophy around objects like this one." → "...objects like this."
- "It was his answer to the question of where beauty actually lives." → "It was his answer to where beauty lives."
- "Not in rare or perfect things made for display, he argued, but in ordinary, handmade, useful ones." → "Not in rare or perfect things made for display, but in ordinary, handmade, useful ones." (mid-sentence attribution clauses can go when the speaker is already clear)
- "no two pieces are ever exactly alike" → "no two pieces are the same"
- "continuing that thread in my own small way" → "continuing that thread in my own way"
- "made for actual daily use" → "made for daily use"

## 2. Avoid technical/spec-sheet language in narrative copy

"Durable" belongs on a product spec line, not in a personal story.

- "durable enough for daily use" → "hard enough for daily use"

## 3. Match register to context

Same fact, described differently depending on where it lives — a product page can be more formal than casual first-person prose.

- "this Blue Teapot" (mid-sentence) → "the blue teapot"
- "hand-thrown, one-of-a-kind" (product-page mode) → "I made this on the wheel, so no two come out quite the same" (prose mode)
- "the studio" referring to itself → "I"

## 4. Keep opposing pairs parallel

Mismatched pairs read as slightly off, even if grammatically fine.

- "outside" paired with "interior" → "outside" paired with "inside"
- "big and small" → not "large and petite" (one side shouldn't get fancier than the other)
- "rough and smooth" → not "textured and refined"

## 5. Present tense for facts still true

Don't default to past tense for something still ongoing today.

- "were inspired by" → "are inspired by"
- "I learned pottery two years ago" → "I've been learning pottery for two years"

## 6. Preserve the undersell

Don't let AI drafts upgrade modesty into confidence. Hedges (*I'd say*, *just*, *a little*) are part of the voice — keep them, don't strip them out even though a generic "polish" pass would remove them.

## 7. Plain connectors

Reach for the plainer, more spoken word.

- "since" → "because" (except where swapping would force restructuring the whole sentence — leave it then)
- "such as" → "like"

## 8. Break up chained "X, Y, and Z" sentences

Don't stack three actions with commas and "and" — split into shorter sentences, and if the last item carries extra trailing detail, split that off too.

- "You center it, throw it up and down, and make a hole in the middle. Then you open it up, shape it with your hands, and finally refine it using a piece of bamboo half-round molding." → "You center it, throw it up and down. When you are done, you make a hole in the middle. Then you open it up, shape it with your hands, and finally refine it. You do this with a bamboo rib."

## 9. Don't inflate a simple fact into overwrought phrasing

State it plainly instead of dressing it up.

- "No two pieces are exactly alike, since each one is shaped by hand and not made from molds." → "Every piece is different, because it is shaped by hand, not made from molds."

(Note: if this exact fact is a recurring line across pieces, use rule 16's standardized wording instead.)

## 10. Fixed terminology for tools and materials

Use the correct English name, not a plausible-sounding invention.

- "bamboo half-round molding" → "bamboo rib" (the standard English name for the tool; "half-round molding" is a construction term for an unrelated thing)

## 11. Don't let the same word echo unintentionally nearby

Deliberate repetition is fine; accidental repetition within a short passage reads as lazy.

- "...fragile and breaks easily... it breaks more easily." → "...it's more fragile."
- "...the shape always drifts slightly... round but not always the same shape... white but not always the same white." → "...the shape drifts slightly..." (drop the first "always")

## 12. No clipped fragments — write full phrases

Don't rely on the reader filling in dropped words.

- "All done freehand, no stencil." → "All done freehand, without a stencil."
- "It's not perfectly round. No moon jar is;" → "No moon jar is perfectly round;"

## 13. No contractions

Always write the full form.

- "here's" → "There is"
- "it's" → "it is"
- "they're" → "they are"

## 14. Stay anchored to the specific object

When describing one piece, don't drift into generalizing about the whole category.

- "No moon jar is perfectly round; **they're** thrown in two halves..." → "No moon jar is perfectly round; **it** is thrown in two halves..." (stay on the piece in front of the reader)

## 15. Non-English words are italicized and glossed on first use

- "...and called it mingei." → "...and called it *mingei* — folk craft."

## 16. Standardize recurring brand lines

When the same statement repeats across pieces, pick one exact wording and reuse it verbatim — don't re-paraphrase it each time it comes up.

- "no two pieces are exactly alike" / "no two are exactly alike" / "no two pieces are ever exactly alike" / "no two pieces are ever quite alike" → pick one: "No two pieces are exactly alike." Use that line everywhere.

## 17. Cut a not-X-but-Y contrast that doesn't correct a real assumption

The negative half should only appear if it corrects something the reader actually believes; otherwise it just adds weight without adding information.

- "Wheel-thrown pottery is ceramic work shaped by hand on a spinning potter's wheel, rather than cast from a mold." → "Wheel-thrown pottery is ceramic work shaped by hand on a spinning potter's wheel." (unless the mold distinction is genuinely useful information for the reader — judgment call, keep it then)
